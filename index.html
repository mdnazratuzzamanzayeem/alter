Why Does Half the Population Bleed in Silence? The Period Chapter Bangladesh's Classrooms Forgot to Teach — And Every Brother, Father, Son, Husband, and Boyfriend Needs to Read

September 9, 2026

Every single month, roughly half the people in Bangladesh and South Asia who are of reproductive age go through something that is completely normal, biologically unremarkable, and utterly essential to human continuation — and yet, somehow, it remains one of the most unspeakable subjects in the region. Not a disease. Not a disorder. A period.

So here's the question worth sitting with for a second: how does something this universal end up this unspoken?

This isn't a "women's issue" tucked away in a corner of health class. It's a subject that touches every household — because every man alive was born from a woman who menstruated, was likely raised alongside a sister, mother, wife, daughter, or friend who does, and will, at some point, be standing in a room where someone he loves is in pain, embarrassed, or simply needs a pack of pads bought without twenty questions attached. And yet, in classrooms across Bangladesh and much of South Asia, the chapter on menstruation is the one page teachers turn past a little too quickly, textbooks mention a little too vaguely, and coaching centers skip entirely because — let's be honest — it was never going to be on the exam.

This blog is an attempt to say the word out loud. Repeatedly. Unapologetically. Because silence has a cost, and it's not the men in the family who are paying it.

The Question Nobody Asks Out Loud

Ask most adult men in Bangladesh to explain what actually happens during a menstrual cycle, and you'll get a shrug, a nervous laugh, or a vague reference to "that time of the month." Ask them why their sister, wife, or daughter sometimes doubles over in pain, avoids the kitchen, or goes quiet for a few days, and the answer is often silence dressed up as respect — as if not knowing is somehow more polite than knowing and caring.

That silence isn't accidental. It's inherited. It's been passed down the way old furniture is passed down — nobody remembers why it's shaped that way, but nobody rearranges it either.

"Shorom-Lojja": The Shame We Inherited, Not Chose

Bangladeshi researchers studying menstrual health in rural schools have a term for this deeply held cultural instinct toward silence and modesty: shorom-lojja — a culturally enforced norm of shame and restraint that governs what can and cannot be said, especially about the body, especially by or in front of women. It's not written into any law. It doesn't need to be. It lives in the way mothers whisper instructions to daughters, in the way a stained cloth is hidden rather than washed openly, in the way a girl learns to apologize with her body language before she's even done anything wrong.

Researchers studying rural communities across Bangladesh and South Asia have documented an entire ecosystem of restrictions that follow a menstruating woman or girl: she may be kept from entering the kitchen, discouraged from going outside, restricted from bathing normally, or even avoided physically by others in the household — practices rooted in old beliefs that treat menstruation less like biology and more like a contagion or a curse. None of these restrictions have any grounding in medicine. All of them have staying power because nobody — least of all the men in the house — was ever taught to question them.

The Textbook Chapter That Exists on Paper Only

Here's where it gets almost absurd: menstruation is technically in the curriculum. Governments have written it into national frameworks. Biology books have a page for it. And yet, study after study on Bangladeshi classrooms finds the same pattern — the subject is "biologically recognized but socially censored." It exists on paper. It rarely survives contact with an actual classroom.

A few things researchers have consistently found:

  • In a nationally cited survey, only around 6 percent of schools in Bangladesh were found to provide any real health and hygiene education at all — the topic exists in policy far more than it exists in practice.
  • A study analyzing anonymous questions submitted by Bangladeshi schoolchildren found that current textbooks largely exclude the practical, real-life side of menstruation — how to actually manage it, what's normal, what to do when it starts unexpectedly — leaving students to fill the gaps with rumor, fear, or silence.
  • Teachers themselves openly admit the problem. In interviews and pilot studies across Bangladeshi schools, teachers have described feeling embarrassed, underprepared, and unsupported when it comes to teaching puberty and menstruation — some unsure how to even respond to common myths, like the belief that a menstruating girl shouldn't cook.
  • One research team studying Bangladeshi teachers went as far as recommending that menstrual and puberty education be tied directly to national examinations — on the theory that if it isn't tested, it simply won't be taught with any seriousness. In an education culture built around exams and coaching centers, a chapter with no marks attached is a chapter with no urgency attached.

That last point deserves its own sentence: in a system where coaching centers exist to drill students on exactly what will be asked and nothing else, an untested chapter is functionally an invisible chapter. It's not that the information doesn't exist. It's that the entire architecture of exam-driven education has quietly decided it doesn't matter enough to teach properly — in school, in college, or in the coaching classes that shape so much of a student's real education in this region.

The Numbers Behind the Whisper

Statistics rarely capture lived experience, but they're useful for showing just how widespread this "personal" problem actually is.

  • Only about 32 percent of girls in Bangladesh knew what menstruation was before it happened to them for the first time, according to UNICEF-cited national data — meaning roughly two out of three girls experience their first period with little to no idea what's happening to their own body.
  • Of those caught off guard, 69 percent said they felt scared the first time it happened — not curious, not neutral, but frightened, in a moment that should have been met with information and reassurance instead.
  • Nearly one in three girls in Bangladesh miss around two and a half days of school every month because of their periods — a monthly, recurring loss of education driven not by illness, but by stigma and a lack of safe, private facilities.
  • More than half of women in Bangladesh — and more than two-thirds of women in neighboring Nepal — report that they don't participate in ordinary daily activities while menstruating, whether that's cooking, socializing, praying, or simply moving freely through their own home.
  • Globally, an estimated 500 million people lack adequate access to menstrual products and the facilities needed to manage their periods safely and with dignity — and South Asia carries a heavy share of that number.

These aren't abstract figures. Each percentage point is a girl sitting out of class, a woman quietly excusing herself from a family gathering, a wife enduring pain nobody in the house has been taught to notice.

What Silence Actually Costs

The consequences of this silence aren't limited to embarrassment. They compound.

Lost education. Missing multiple days of school every month, semester after semester, year after year, adds up to a real, measurable education gap — one that disproportionately affects girls' futures, confidence, and independence.

Health risks. Without accurate information or affordable products, many women and girls in Bangladesh — especially in flood-affected and rural areas — resort to using old cloth, rags, or newspaper during their periods. Improperly washed or dried materials, often hidden out of shame rather than dried openly in the sun, have been linked to urinary and reproductive infections. During floods and emergencies, the problem intensifies sharply, as sanitation facilities disappear entirely.

A supply chain built on shame, not logistics. One story shared by UNICEF Bangladesh captures this perfectly: because most shopkeepers selling sanitary products are men, a girl who needs pads often cannot buy them herself. She tells her mother. Her mother tells the father. The father buys the pads and hands them back to the mother, who finally gives them to the daughter. If any one link in that chain forgets, hesitates, or is unavailable — the girl simply goes without. A basic hygiene product, gated behind three layers of second-hand embarrassment, entirely because one man in a shop and one man in the house were never taught that this is just... a purchase.

Emotional weight. Fear, shame, and isolation during a recurring, unavoidable biological process take a quiet mental toll — one rarely acknowledged because acknowledging it would require talking about the thing itself.

"Not a Girls' Issue": The Case for Every Man in the Room

This is usually where these conversations get filed away — as something for mothers to explain to daughters, women to discuss among themselves, "not really a men's topic." But that framing is exactly the problem, and it falls apart the moment you look at who's actually in the room when it matters.

For the Brother

A sister's first period often arrives with fear before it arrives with understanding — remember, roughly 7 in 10 Bangladeshi girls describe feeling scared, not informed. A brother who understands what's happening isn't a bystander to that fear; he's often the one who can normalize it with a shrug instead of a smirk, who can hand over money for pads without turning it into a whispered family drama, who doesn't make a sister feel like she's done something wrong by simply existing in her own body that week.

For the Dad Raising a Daughter (or a Son)

A father who understands menstruation can be the difference between a daughter who feels prepared and one who feels ambushed by her own body. He can be the one who makes sure pads are always in the house before they're urgently needed, who doesn't flinch or delegate the topic entirely to "her mother's job," who models — for a son watching, too — that this is a normal biological fact, not a shameful secret to be managed around.

For the Son Who Grew Up Watching His Mother Hide

Many men grew up watching their mothers manage periods in total secrecy — hiding cloth, whispering explanations, absorbing pain quietly so as not to "bother" anyone. A son who understands what his mother actually goes through, and why she sometimes needs rest, quiet, or simply less to carry that week, can offer something rare: care without being asked, because he finally understands what he's caring about.

For the Husband

A husband who understands the menstrual cycle isn't just being thoughtful — he's building a marriage on actual understanding rather than tolerated mystery. Recognizing period pain as real physical pain (not moodiness to be managed around), knowing when rest is needed rather than resented, and being able to simply pick up pads at the store without ceremony all quietly signal something bigger: that his wife's body isn't a topic too awkward for him to know about.

For the Boyfriend and the Friend

Outside of family, this matters just as much. A boyfriend or close friend who understands periods can be the difference between a partner who feels safe mentioning she's in pain, and one who learns to hide it because past reactions taught her it wasn't worth mentioning. Small things — not treating the topic as disgusting, not joking about it at someone's expense, offering a hot water bottle or simply asking "do you need anything?" — build the kind of trust that most South Asian relationships are never explicitly taught to build around this subject.

None of this requires a medical degree. It requires basic, accurate knowledge and the willingness to treat a normal bodily process as exactly that — normal.

What Changing the Story Actually Looks Like

The good news is that this isn't being ignored everywhere. In 2021, UNICEF worked with the Government of Bangladesh to develop a National Menstrual Hygiene Management Strategy, explicitly designed to weave menstrual education into school lessons for both girls and boys — not as a separate, hidden lesson for one gender, but as shared, ordinary knowledge. Efforts are underway to build private, hygienic school facilities, install sanitary pad vending machines in health centers, and train teachers to handle the subject with less discomfort and more confidence.

Public health researchers have also pushed for a very specific, very practical fix: tie menstrual and puberty education to the exam system itself. If a subject is tested, coaching centers teach it, teachers prepare for it, and students take it seriously — the same logic that currently keeps this chapter out of serious study could just as easily bring it in.

But policy alone doesn't rewrite culture. That part happens at home, one conversation at a time — often started by the men willing to ask the "awkward" question instead of avoiding it.

A Short, Honest To-Do List for the Men Reading This

  • Learn the basics. You don't need a textbook — a five-minute honest search on what a menstrual cycle actually is will put you ahead of most men in your position.
  • Normalize the word. Say "period" instead of a euphemism whispered behind a hand. Language sets the tone for how seriously something is taken.
  • Keep supplies stocked, no announcement needed. If there's a woman in your household, having pads available before they're urgently needed removes one small daily source of stress.
  • Buy them yourself, without ceremony. Walking up to a counter and buying sanitary pads is not embarrassing. Treat it exactly as ordinary as buying soap — because it is.
  • Don't dismiss the pain. Period pain can be genuinely severe. "You're fine" is rarely the right response; "do you need rest, heat, or medicine?" usually is.
  • Correct the myths when you hear them. If someone repeats an old belief about menstruating women being "unclean" or unable to cook, that's a small, low-stakes moment to gently push back.
  • Ask, don't assume. Every woman's experience with her cycle is different. A little curiosity, expressed respectfully, goes further than silence ever will.
  • Talk to the next generation. If you're raising a son, teach him this early. If you're raising a daughter, make sure fear isn't her first teacher.

The Last Word

There is nothing shameful about a biological process that every single human being on this planet exists because of. What's genuinely embarrassing is a curriculum that skips it, a coaching culture that ignores it because it isn't graded, and generations of men who were never given the chance to simply know — and so learned to look away instead.

Breaking a silence this old doesn't require a grand gesture. It requires brothers who don't flinch, fathers who prepare instead of avoid, sons who understand what their mothers carried quietly, husbands who show up with more than tolerance, and friends who make space instead of jokes.

Half the population bleeds. It's long past time the other half stopped pretending not to notice.

Free Tabs for SSC Toppers: A Good Idea Bangladesh Has Failed to Execute Before — Will 2026 Be Different?

September 9, 2026

A look at the newly announced device giveaway for SSC 2026's GPA-5 achievers, read against the country's track record with the Doel laptop and its unresolved e-waste crisis.

What was actually announced

On September 1, 2026, Education Minister ANM Ehsanul Haque Milon told a University Day audience at Khulna University of Engineering & Technology that the government would give tablets to the roughly 116,000 students who scored GPA-5 in this year's SSC and equivalent exams, framing it as recognition for meritorious students and a push toward tech-enabled learning. Within hours, on his verified Facebook page the same night, the minister revised the plan: laptops, not tablets, and only for students who hit a "Golden GPA-5" — at least 80 marks in every single subject — a narrower group whose exact size the education boards haven't disclosed.

That reversal, inside a single evening, is worth sitting with. It means the device, the eligibility bar, the recipient count, the brand, the specifications, the procurement method and the budget were all still unsettled at the moment the announcement made headlines. Nothing about a tender, a supplier, or a per-unit cost has been made public as of this writing.

This year's SSC results also came in weaker than usual: the pass rate fell to 62.25% from roughly 68% the year before, and GPA-5 holders dropped from 139,032 in 2025 to 116,676 in 2026, per Business Standard's coverage of the results. Some context worth keeping in view as the country debates spending public money on a hardware giveaway.

Bangladesh has run this experiment before — and it didn't go well

The most direct precedent isn't a giveaway to SSC toppers specifically — no earlier scheme of that exact shape appears to be on record — but Bangladesh's broader history of state-driven, budget device programs offers a clear warning.

The Doel laptop project, launched by Telephone Shilpa Sangstha (TSS) in 2011, was the government's flagship attempt to build an affordable, locally assembled computer and get it into schools, post offices, and the army. It collapsed. Academic case studies of the project found that its two main technical/investment partners abandoned the venture within about a year of launch, that by 2013 barely two-thirds of the laptops assembled had actually been sold, and that a large share of the buyers were the Ministry of Education itself. A university-published market study found that 83% of users who responded to surveys reported battery problems, alongside recurring complaints about Wi-Fi, webcams, RAM and screen resolution. The Daily Star later reported that the education ministry, the postal department and the army — the project's three big institutional buyers — all lodged complaints about build quality. Manufacturing was effectively wound down by 2016, and in 2026 the state minister for ICT was still on record asking for an inquiry into who should be held accountable for the project's failure, according to Bangla tech outlet Jagonews24.

Separately, the government has repeatedly turned to domestic manufacturer Walton for large device procurements — 25,125 laptops for an ICT Division project, and a roughly Tk447 crore contract for 395,000 tablets for the 2021 digital census, among others. These were competitively tendered public purchases rather than free giveaways to students, and there's no comparable independent quality audit of them on the public record the way there is for Doel. But they establish that "buy Bangladeshi, buy cheap, buy at scale for a state program" is a familiar procurement instinct here — one that hasn't always been paired with a public accounting of how the devices performed once delivered.

None of this means the 2026 device giveaway is destined to repeat Doel's fate. It means the country has direct, documented experience with exactly this failure mode, and that experience is the reasonable baseline against which to judge a new, still-unspecified program.

The part nobody's talking yet: what happens to these devices in three years

This is where the "e-waste" question stops being rhetorical. Bangladesh already has a serious, well-documented e-waste problem, and the enforcement mechanism meant to manage it is widely reported as barely functioning.

A few figures worth knowing, drawn from recent research and a 2026 field assessment:

  • Bangladesh generates an estimated 3 million tonnes of e-waste annually, and academic reviews describe the sector as growing roughly 20% a year.
  • Less than 10% of that e-waste is formally recycled, according to a 2026 assessment by the digital-rights group VOICE (Voices for Interactive Choice and Empowerment), conducted with the Association for Progressive Communications.
  • The same VOICE assessment found that of 15 surveyed entities, none were actually collecting end-of-life products as required.
  • Bangladesh loses an estimated US$200–221 million a year in recoverable material value — gold, copper, and other components — because so little e-waste is properly processed, per that report.
  • The country does have a legal framework: the Hazardous Waste (E-Waste) Management Rules, 2021, issued under the Bangladesh Environmental Protection Act, 1995, which sets escalating collection targets for manufacturers and importers and carries penalties of up to two years' imprisonment or a Tk200,000 fine for violations. But researchers reviewing its rollout have consistently found a wide gap between what the rules require on paper and what happens on the ground.

Put those two facts side by side. A government that already has trouble getting the industry to take back and safely process ordinary consumer electronics is about to inject well over 100,000 new devices — tablets or laptops, whichever it settles on — into households across the country, with no public statement yet about warranty length, repair support, or what happens to the unit once it dies. A cheaply sourced device with a two- or three-year real-world lifespan, multiplied across six-figure recipient numbers, is not a hypothetical addition to that waste stream — it's an entirely foreseeable one, and foreseeable is exactly the point at which planning is supposed to happen, not after the fact.

Will the hardware actually be usable — or a repeat of the spec sheet that sank Doel?

There's a second, more basic question sitting underneath all the procurement and e-waste concerns above: even if a device does ship on time, will it be fast enough to actually be worth using?

This isn't a hypothetical worry. Doel's own published spec sheets from 2011 are on record, and they're instructive. The entry "Primary" model — the one the education ministry itself bought in bulk — ran on a VIA 8650 processor clocked at 800MHz, paired with just 512MB of RAM and 16GB of flash storage in place of a hard drive. The step-up "Basic" model wasn't much better: an Intel Atom N455 at 1.66GHz, 1GB of RAM, and a 250GB HDD. Atom-class netbook chips like these were built for basic web browsing at launch and were already considered sluggish for everyday multitasking within a couple of years — a machine like that trying to run a modern browser with a dozen tabs, a video call, and a word processor open at once today would grind to a crawl. That's the real-world version of the "unusable, painful to use" scenario worth naming plainly: a free laptop that's too slow to actually do coursework, research, or exam prep on is not meaningfully different from no laptop at all — arguably worse, since it creates the appearance that the digital-divide problem has been solved when it hasn't.

So what does "usable" actually mean by 2026 standards, and how does that compare to a low-spec device like Core 2 Duo-class processors, 4GB of RAM, and mechanical hard drives — the tier this kind of budget program has tended to land on historically?

  • Processor: Old dual-core chips from the Core 2 Duo era are more than a decade and a half old, lack modern power-efficiency and security features, and are no longer considered adequate for a current OS plus a browser doing real work. Even an entry-level 2026 laptop is expected to ship with a current-generation quad-core-or-better mobile chip.
  • RAM: Microsoft's official floor for Windows 11 is 4GB, but hardware guides and Microsoft's own engineers describe that as barely enough to boot, with 8GB as the practical minimum for smooth everyday use and 16GB increasingly treated as the sensible baseline for a machine expected to last several years, per HP's 2026 buying guidance and independent hardware coverage. A 4GB machine handed to a student today would struggle from day one, not just after a few years of wear.
  • Storage: A mechanical hard drive — 512GB or otherwise — is now considered the single biggest bottleneck in an otherwise decent machine; SSD storage is the baseline expectation on any laptop sold as new in 2026, because it affects boot time, app responsiveness and overall feel far more than capacity does.

None of this requires an expensive machine. Genuinely usable, SSD-based, 8GB-RAM laptops exist at prices well within the range Doel itself charged for its worst-performing models in 2011 taka-adjusted terms. The risk isn't that a decent laptop is unaffordable — it's that "cheap" and "outdated" quietly get treated as the same purchasing decision, the way they were with Doel's bottom-tier models. That's exactly why the specifications need to be published before delivery, not discovered by students unboxing their prize: a processor and RAM figure on a tender document is a two-line disclosure, and it's the single fastest way to tell whether this program is a genuine tool or a repeat of a machine nobody could actually use.

The fair case for the program

It's worth stating plainly: the underlying idea isn't unreasonable, and dismissing it outright would be its own kind of unfairness. Rewarding academic merit is a normal thing for an education system to do, and putting a computer in the hands of a strong student from a low-income household can be a genuine, life-changing form of access — to research, to university applications, to the "AI era" skills the government has been publicly emphasizing since Prime Minister Tarique Rahman's inaugural address in February. A tablet or laptop is also a more modern, arguably more useful prize than the medals, certificates or cash stipends such programs have traditionally used. None of the concerns above are an argument against helping meritorious students. They're an argument for doing it with a plan.

What "doing it with a plan" would actually require

Before public money is committed, there's a fairly short list of things a transparent version of this program would need to disclose — and that journalists, opposition lawmakers, and civil-society watchdogs like Transparency International Bangladesh would be well within their normal, legitimate role to keep asking about:

  • Procurement method — open competitive tender or single-source purchase, and from which supplier(s)
  • Published technical specifications — not just "tablet" or "laptop," but the processor, RAM, storage type (SSD vs HDD) and build-quality standard being paid for, benchmarked against what a 2026 machine needs to actually run smoothly rather than merely boot
  • Total budget and per-unit cost, so the public can judge value for money against comparable commercial devices
  • Warranty and after-sales support terms, including who repairs a broken unit and for how long
  • A stated device lifespan and a genuine end-of-life plan — ideally a manufacturer or government take-back commitment consistent with the 2021 e-waste rules, not a vague aspiration
  • An independent quality audit at delivery, given that the last comparable domestic hardware push required a still-unresolved government inquiry into what went wrong

None of this is an accusation that officials intend to repeat Doel's mistakes or that any individual has acted improperly — nothing in the public record supports that, and the procurement hasn't even happened yet. It's simply what "learning the lesson" looks like in concrete terms, for a country that has already paid once, in money and credibility, for skipping these steps.

The bottom line

Bangladesh doesn't have to choose between rewarding its top students and avoiding another Doel. The choice the government actually faces is narrower and more boring: publish the specifications, publish the cost, and say — before the devices ship — what happens to them when they stop working. That's not a high bar, and it's also not one the country has consistently cleared before. Given that history, expecting the details up front isn't cynicism about this specific announcement. It's the ordinary due diligence a taxpayer-funded program of this size should get regardless of which party is in office.

The Silent Collapse

July 9, 2026

How the HSC Exam Debacle Exposed the Broken Foundation of Bangladesh’s Education System

For decades, we have comforted ourselves with a comforting illusion: that a rising pass rate and an explosion of GPA-5 achievements in our public examinations meant our youth were marching toward a brighter, more competitive future. That illusion has shattered.

The publication of the 2025 Higher Secondary Certificate (HSC) and equivalent exam results sent shockwaves across Bangladesh. It wasn't just a poor showing; it was an absolute debacle—the lowest pass rate in 21 years. Over half a million young minds walked away from this crucial milestone having failed, leaving families despondent, educators scrambling, and the entire nation facing an uncomfortable truth: our education system is in a state of quiet, systemic collapse.

The Anatomy of a Debacle: 2025 by the Numbers

To understand how deep this wound runs, we have to look past the political rhetoric and examine the stark data of the latest results. The drop-off from previous years isn't just a minor statistical fluctuation—it is a vertical cliff.

Metric 2024 / Previous Years 2025 Results The Reality Shift

GPA-5 Achievers ~131,376 (2024 general) 63,219 A reduction of over 77,000 top-tier scores.

"This is the worst result in 21 years. It is frightening for our future. We need to discuss why this is happening."

Why Did the System Fail the Class of 2025?

This catastrophic drop didn't happen in a vacuum. It is the cumulative fallout of years of policy instability, natural disasters, and structural failures that the system simply refused to adapt to.

1. The English and ICT "Gatekeeper" Trap

An analysis of the failures reveals that the massive drop-off was heavily driven by disastrous performances in two core subjects: English and Information and Communication Technology (ICT).

For years, the curriculum has forced students to memorize grammar structures and outdated ICT definitions rather than teaching them how to write practically or understand technology. When examiners stopped using highly predictable questions or evaluation standards shifted slightly closer to actual merit, the fragile deck of cards collapsed. Students who could comfortably memorize a guide-book paragraph were left entirely unable to write an original sentence.

2. The Unaddressed COVID-19 Hangover

We love to pretend that the pandemic is ancient history, but for the 18-to-20-year-olds taking the HSC today, it was the defining disruption of their educational foundation.

During crucial early-development years, these students lost nearly two years of classroom continuity. Online classes were a luxury enjoyed by a tiny, urban elite; the rest of the country received practically no real learning. The "learning gap" was never repaired. Instead of implementing robust remedial learning programs, the state simply pushed these students through the system, expecting them to magically sit for standard, rigorous board exams.

3. Absolute Chaos and Exam Disruptions

The academic life of an HSC candidate in Bangladesh over the last year has been defined by anxiety. From severe natural disasters—such as the massive monsoon floods that forced the sudden suspension of exams in Chattogram—to political instability, the environment has been anything but conducive to academic focus.

Students have had to march through knee-deep water to reach exam halls, wait out delayed schedules, and deal with intense social stress. The psychological toll of preparing under such volatile conditions is immeasurable, directly resulting in cognitive exhaustion and exam-room failures.

The Deeper, Structural Rot

To blame the HSC debacle solely on "one bad year" or "tough questions" is a cop-out. The HSC results are merely a symptom; the disease is the systematic decay of the entire Bangladeshi educational framework.

The Tyranny of Rote Learning and the "Coaching Class" Complex

We have created an education system that values certificates over competence, and grades over actual learning. From primary school to college, students are trained to be mimicry machines.

The Cycle: Wake up at 6:00 AM → attend college (where little actual teaching happens) → run to private tutors → spend evenings at coaching centers → memorize guidebooks late into the night.

The Result: The moment these "GPA-5" students step into a university exam hall, the illusion fades. Up to 91.5% of students fail university admission tests despite high secondary GPAs. When they enter the workforce, employers complain that university graduates cannot write a basic corporate email or structure a simple analytical report.

The Grand Urban-Rural Chasm

If you reside in a posh neighborhood in Dhaka, you have access to high-speed internet, experienced teachers, and air-conditioned coaching centers. If you are a student in rural Feni, Noakhali, or Barishal, you are likely studying in a school that suffers from daily power outages, lacks basic internet facilities, and has a severe shortage of trained subject teachers.

Yet, the government tests both students on the exact same exam script. The "lowest pass rate in 21 years" is heavily weighted by the absolute abandonment of rural schools, where pass rates in some colleges hit an absolute zero.

Severe Financial Starvation

While UNESCO recommends that developing nations allocate 4% to 6% of their GDP to education, Bangladesh routinely allocates a meager 1.8%. Worse, due to budget cuts and bureaucratic implementation failures, the actual expenditure often drops to a humiliating 1.3%.

To make matters worse, 80% to 90% of this budget goes directly to administrative salaries. There is practically nothing left over to modernize classrooms, purchase technology, build digital infrastructure, or offer competitive salaries to attract brilliant, qualified minds into the teaching profession.

How to Save Our Future: A Plan for Radical Reform

If we do not act immediately, we will produce a generation of degree-holders who are functionally unemployed, unable to compete in an AI-driven global economy. Saving our education system requires moving past cosmetic band-aids. We need structural surgery:

1. Ban Guidebooks and Dismantle the Coaching Industry

We must completely outlaw the commercial publication of exam-shortcut guidebooks. Textbooks must be redesigned to be interactive, comprehensive, and connected to real-life applications. Classrooms must become the primary source of education again, breaking the economic stranglehold that predatory coaching centers have on middle-class families.

2. Shift Focus from Final Exams to Continuous Assessment

Relying on a single, high-stakes 3-hour exam to determine a student’s entire life path is archaic and cruel. We must transition to a continuous assessment model. Projects, presentations, critical writing, and consistent class performance should carry equal weight, teaching students how to think rather than what to memorize.

3. Elevate the Teacher’s Status and Training

A curriculum is only as good as the person delivering it. We must depoliticize school management committees and establish an independent, permanent Education Commission. Teachers must receive rigorous, continuous training. Most importantly, teaching must be made a financially rewarding career path to attract top-tier university graduates.

4. Realignment of the Academic Calendar

Our current academic schedule is plagued by wasted "dead time" caused by administrative delays, overlapping exam sessions, and sudden political or climate disruptions. Restructuring the academic calendar to run on a tight, predictable schedule can reclaim up to a year of lost time for our youth, reducing national stress and boosting learning hours.

The Wake-Up Call We Needed

The disastrous HSC results are painful, but they are also a profound gift. They have stripped away the lies we told ourselves and exposed the structural decay before it is completely too late to fix.

Our youth have shown extraordinary resilience through pandemics, political upheavals, and natural crises. They deserve a state that matches their resilience with responsibility. It is time to step off the GPA-chasing treadmill, invest heavily in actual learning, and rebuild Bangladesh’s education system on a foundation of critical thinking, equity, and genuine competence.

If we don't, our tomorrow will be a nation rich in certificates, but bankrupt in intellect.
